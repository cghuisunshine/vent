Learning goals

By the end, you can:

  • Explain the “entrepreneurial myth” in practical terms.
  • Identify the three internal roles: Entrepreneur, Manager, Technician.
  • Draft a simple franchise prototype: a business that works predictably without heroics.
  • Create a starter operating system (SOPs, checklists, metrics, training plan).

The core problem: confusing doing with building

The pattern

  • You’re great at the craft (the work itself).
  • The business grows, and you become the bottleneck.
  • Stress rises, quality becomes inconsistent, and “everything depends on me.”

The reframe

  • Your job becomes: build a machine that produces results, not personally produce every result.

The three roles inside every operator

Technician

  • Loves doing the work.
  • Optimizes for speed and immediate output.
  • Risk: builds a job, not a business.

Manager

  • Loves order, plans, and predictability.
  • Turns chaos into schedules, staffing, and checklists.
  • Risk: becomes the “cop” without clear systems.

Entrepreneur

  • Loves vision, change, possibility.
  • Designs the future: positioning, product mix, growth path.
  • Risk: constant reinvention without execution.

Practical takeaway

  • Schedule time for all three roles.
  • If one role dominates, you get predictable failure modes (burnout, stagnation, or chaos).

The franchise prototype (repeatability as a feature)

What “franchise-like” means here

  • Not “open a franchise,” but “make your operation teachable and repeatable.”
  • A new hire can deliver acceptable quality with minimal tribal knowledge.

Why it matters

  • Repeatability increases quality, profitability, and your ability to step away without collapse.

The Business Development Process (a usable sequence)

Step 1 — Primary Aim (your life, not your business)

  • What kind of life do you want (time, stress, money, meaning)?
  • What does “success” look like in a normal week?

Step 2 — Strategic Objective (target state)

  • Target customer
  • Offering (what you sell)
  • Differentiator (why you)
  • Revenue/profit targets
  • Owner-independence target (how much runs without you)

Step 3 — Organizational Strategy (roles before people)

Create an org chart with seats (one person can hold multiple seats today):

  • Owner/CEO
  • Operations
  • Sales/Marketing
  • Finance/Admin
  • Delivery/Service

Rule: design the organization you need, then decide who fills seats.

Step 4 — Management Strategy (cadence + accountability)

  • Weekly planning + review meeting
  • Daily/shift check-ins if needed
  • Clear owners for each metric and process

Step 5 — People Strategy (hiring + training)

  • Role scorecards (outcomes, behaviors, skills)
  • Training path (week 1, week 2, 30 days)
  • Feedback rhythm (weekly early, then monthly)

Step 6 — Marketing Strategy (repeatable pipeline)

  • Target segment and promise
  • Lead sources (2–4 you’ll commit to)
  • Conversion steps (scripts, offers, follow-up)
  • Retention/referrals plan

Step 7 — Systems Strategy (the operating manual)

Build systems that make results predictable.

Systems: what to document first

Start with high-frequency, high-risk processes

Pick 5–10 processes that most affect customer experience and cash flow:

  • Opening/closing routine
  • Handling inquiries/leads
  • Delivering the core service
  • Quality checks and rework handling
  • Billing, refunds, complaints
  • Inventory/order management (if relevant)

SOP format (keep it teachable)

  • Purpose
  • Trigger
  • Inputs
  • Steps (numbered, observable)
  • Quality standard (“done means…”)
  • Time expectation
  • Exceptions

Metrics: the minimum dashboard

Choose a few leading and lagging indicators:

  • Leads/week (leading)
  • Conversion rate (leading)
  • On-time delivery rate (leading)
  • Rework/defect rate (leading)
  • Revenue and gross margin (lagging)
  • Cash on hand (lagging)

Rule: if you can’t explain how a metric changes behavior, don’t track it yet.

A 4-week implementation plan (starter system)

Week 1 — Clarify roles and cadence

  • Draft the org chart (seats, not names).
  • Schedule a weekly review: metrics + priorities + blockers.
  • Write the Primary Aim and Strategic Objective (1 page each).

Week 2 — Document the first 3 SOPs

  • Choose the three most repeated processes.
  • Create checklists a new hire could follow.
  • Add a “quality gate” to each (what must be true to call it done).

Week 3 — Train and test

  • Run SOPs as if you were new.
  • Note friction points and rewrite for clarity.
  • Start a simple dashboard (spreadsheet is fine).

Week 4 — Strengthen the pipeline

  • Document lead handling and follow-up steps.
  • Create a basic onboarding plan (first 5 shifts / first 2 weeks).
  • Identify one constraint and improve it.

Quiz (self-check)

  1. Why does a great Technician often struggle as the business grows?
  2. What does “org chart with seats” mean?
  3. Name one leading metric and one lagging metric for your context.
  4. What makes an SOP teachable?
  5. What’s one risk of Entrepreneur-only behavior?

Answer key (short)

  1. The business becomes dependent on personal output and decisions.
  2. Roles are designed before choosing people; one person can hold multiple seats.
  3. Example: leads/week (leading), revenue (lagging).
  4. Clear triggers, observable steps, and defined quality standards.
  5. Constant change without stable execution.
